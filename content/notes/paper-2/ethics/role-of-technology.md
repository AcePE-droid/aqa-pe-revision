## Sports Analytics and Data Collection

**Research** is a systematic process of investigation carried out to advance knowledge, and **sports analytics** is the study of data from sports performances using analytical tools to improve results, e.g. **Opta**'s statistical analysis in cricket and football, or GPS analysis showing distances and speeds covered by rugby/football players.

- **Quantitative data** is factual, numerical information gathered through formal, objective research, e.g. a VO2 max test; **qualitative data** deals in words and is subjective, concerning feelings and opinions, e.g. coaches judging a gymnastics routine – qualitative data can be less precise and more time-consuming to collect
- **Objective data** is fact-based, measurable and observable, e.g. performance analysis at the EIS; **subjective data** rests on personal opinion and is less measurable and meaningful, e.g. a parent's half-time comments

## Validity and Reliability

- **Validity** is the degree to which data actually measures what it claims to measure, and whether conclusions drawn from it are appropriate
- **Reliability** means data is consistent, producing similar results when repeated – threats include researchers misusing equipment (e.g. skinfold calipers) or poorly maintained equipment
- Validity is a prerequisite for reliability – if data is not valid, its reliability is questionable

## Video and Analysis Programmes

**Video motion analysis** obtains information about moving objects from video, typically using a high-speed camera and computer software for frame-by-frame playback, e.g. correcting technique or ball release angle.

- **Notational match analysis** records movement patterns, strategy and tactics; **biomechanics** (or **kinematics**) analyses the sporting impact of body movements through quantitative study of motion, displacement, velocity and speed
- **Performance analysis (PA)** provides objective feedback to a performer – an EIS report found athletes/coaches recall only around 30% of a performance correctly, so PA supplies the missing 70%
- **Dartfish** allows users to view, edit and analyse video of technique; **Prozone** tags player actions in football using high-definition cameras

## Testing and Recording Equipment: Indirect Calorimetry and the Metabolic Cart

**Calorimetry** measures heat/energy eliminated or stored in a system. **Indirect calorimetry** measures oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production, translating them into a heat equivalent – it is non-invasive and relatively accurate.

- The **metabolic cart** is the equipment used, producing **resting energy expenditure (REE)** – the energy required by the body over 24 hours at rest, which varies with weight, age, gender and body chemistry (e.g. smoking raises REE)
- It is used to determine energy requirements, calculate nutritional needs, and support obese individuals
- Sources of error can be remembered via **LOSE**: **L**eakage of air; **O**verfeeding/underfeeding based on results; **S**ingle snapshots being less reliable than longer studies; **E**rrors in measurement or recording

## GPS and Motion-Tracking Software and Hardware

**GPS** is a space-based navigation system providing location and time information, tracking speed, distance and direction – in contact sports it can measure impact in **'G' forces**.

- GPS supports objective decisions on substitutions, gauges fatigue to reduce injury risk, and manages workload for players returning from injury
- **Strava** shows GPS technology benefiting athletes at all levels, connecting them worldwide to compare and compete

## Maintaining Data Integrity

**Data integrity** means maintaining the accuracy and consistency of data across its lifetime.

- It can be compromised by human error, transmission errors, software bugs or viruses, and hardware malfunction
- Threats are minimised by regular backups, controlling access, designing interfaces that prevent invalid input, and using error detection/correction software

## Functions of Sports Analytics

- **Monitoring fitness for performance**: at recreational level, monitoring heart rate or calories; at elite level, monitoring distance, pace, sleep and heart rate via smart wearable devices
- **Aiding skill and technique development**: video programmes like Dartfish; **electrostimulation devices** aid rehabilitation and prevent muscle atrophy, but must not be over-used due to a saturation effect
- **Game analysis**: real-time data on iPads, GPS receivers measuring distance and acceleration, helping coaches with tactics and substitutions – quality of information matters more than quantity
- **Talent identification and scouting**: qualitative scouting reports and **pathway analytics** benchmarking performance pathways, e.g. 'Tall and Talented' for sports requiring height, such as basketball and rowing

## Development of Equipment: Adapted Equipment for the Elderly and People with Disabilities

Assistive/adapted technology is increasing, e.g. specially designed wheelchairs and throwing frames in athletics, and cross-country sit skis.

- Wheelchair design is sport-specific: lightweight frames for tennis/basketball; reinforced frames for contact sports like rugby; bucket seats and T-frames for racing chairs
- Adapted equipment for the elderly includes finger extension exercisers, low-impact pedal machines, and exercise balance beams; **prosthetics** such as the Springlite device assist running through improved gait efficiency

## Development of Facilities: The Olympic Legacy

**'Places People Play'** is a Sport England initiative raising participation by providing community sports facilities, including its **'Iconic Facilities'** strand funding strategic, high-quality, multi-sport projects.

- **3G surfaces** are sand and rubber infilled synthetic pitches suited to hockey, football and rugby, supporting frequent use and consistent conditions, though criticised as too rigid for joints and ligaments – newer 3G/4G surfaces have improved this, e.g. Widnes Vikings and Saracens playing on artificial turf
- **Multi-use games areas (MUGAs)** provide multi-sport opportunities where dedicated single-sport surfaces are too expensive; the **Lee Valley VeloPark** is a specialist Olympic legacy facility

## Impact of Technology on Sport, Performers, Coaches and the Audience

- Positive: technology delivers **marginal gains** (e.g. Sir Dave Brailsford's heated trousers for British Cycling), increases participation and inclusivity through adapted equipment, and improves playing surfaces and player development data
- Negative: data can mislead without context; cost creates inequality between wealthy and less well-funded teams (e.g. Team Sky's technology partnership with Jaguar); **paralysis by analysis** can reduce creative, instant reactions; and a **placebo effect** can occur with unproven equipment
- For performers: improved clothing (e.g. the Speedo LZR Racer), equipment, protective gear and recovery methods bring benefits, but risks include injury/over-aggression, cheating via masking agents, and expense creating inequality
- For coaches: video analysis (Dartfish, Prozone) enables detailed opponent and player analysis, but over-reliance on data and hacking risks are drawbacks
- For the audience: statistics and 'mic'd up' officials increase engagement and excitement (e.g. Hawkeye), but officiating technology can cause a loss of respect for officials, cost-driven inconsistency, and disruptive breaks in play
