## Social and Psychological Reasons for Doping

**Doping** means the use of banned performance-enhancing substances by competitors in sport. Reasons behind it are both social (the performer's environment) and psychological (the mental effect chased).

- Social reasons: the **win-at-all-costs** mentality; the scale of **extrinsic rewards** on offer; pressure from coaches, family and media; weak deterrents and confidence that detection can be avoided; and poor role models in certain sports normalising doping
- Psychological reasons: **beta-blockers** to settle nerves where fine motor control decides the outcome (e.g. snooker); **anabolic steroids** to raise aggression for heavy-contact sports (e.g. rugby); **stimulants** to counter a loss of self-belief and boost confidence at events such as the Olympics
- Exam distinction: **physiological** reasons concern the body's function; **psychological** reasons concern the mind

## Physiological Effects of Drugs on the Performer

- **Anabolic steroids** – artificially manufactured hormones (e.g. THG) that assist protein storage and stimulate muscle growth, producing greater strength, power and leaner body composition; used by power athletes, particularly sprinters; side-effects include liver, heart and immune system damage, acne, and aggression/mood swings
- **Beta-blockers** – calm the performer by blocking adrenaline, steadying nerves and improving accuracy; used in high-precision sports (archery, snooker, golf); side-effects include tiredness from lowered blood pressure and reduced aerobic capacity
- **EPO (erythropoietin)** – a naturally occurring hormone, also made artificially, that raises haemoglobin levels and oxygen-carrying capacity, improving endurance and delaying fatigue; used by endurance performers; side-effects include blood clotting, stroke and, rarely, death – e.g. Lance Armstrong's stripped Tour de France titles, and the BALCO scandal involving THG

## Implications of Drug Taking for the Sport

- Doping damages a sport's reputation and public interest, linking sports firmly to individual cheats, e.g. Justin Gatlin with athletics, Lance Armstrong with cycling – the 2007 'Tour de Farce' is a key example
- Suspicion clouds the achievements of clean athletes (e.g. Chris Froome, Mo Farah), and doping supplies negative role models to developing athletes

## Implications of Drug Taking for the Performer

- Positive: performance genuinely improves, and undetected users can gain fame and fortune
- Negatives: serious health risks (steroids linked to high blood pressure, EPO to heart disease and stroke); reputational damage and social isolation; lost income and sponsorship; legal consequences such as bans, stripped medals or imprisonment (e.g. Marion Jones); and accidental use is still punished, e.g. Alain Baxter losing his Olympic medal after an unknowing positive test

## Strategies for Eliminating Performance-Enhancing Drugs

**UK Anti-Doping (UKAD)** protects UK sport from doping across more than 40 sports; **WADA** is the international foundation (est. 1999) that promotes, co-ordinates and monitors the global fight against drugs in sport.

- Strategies can be remembered via **DOPING**: **D**rug-free culture via education (e.g. UKAD's '100% Me' programme); **O**rganisations working together (UKAD, WADA, NGBs); **P**unishments made harsher; **I**nvestment in testing technology; **N**ame and shame negative role models; **G**uilty performers lose funding and sponsorship
- Stricter procedures include **random testing**, **out-of-competition testing** and the **'whereabouts system'**, and positive role models such as Sir Chris Hoy are used to promote clean sport

## Ongoing Problems in the Fight Against Doping

- Testers struggle to gain immediate access to athletes training overseas; new drugs and **masking agents** are continually developed; drugs can be taken accidentally; and regulations and testing procedures differ between countries and sports, making a unified approach hard to achieve

## Arguments For and Against Legalising Drugs in Sport

- For legalisation: the battle against doping is expensive and time-consuming; drugs are easy to access and hard to eliminate; detection is not always effective; defining a 'drug' against a legal supplement is difficult; drugs are sometimes taken accidentally; it is a matter of personal choice; and if everyone doped it would level the playing field
- Against legalisation: serious health risks; negative role models for young people; a negative image for particular sports; increased pressure to dope; sporting success should rest on hard work and natural talent; drugs confer an unfair, immoral advantage and are cheating; only wealthier countries could afford them; and it is illegal
