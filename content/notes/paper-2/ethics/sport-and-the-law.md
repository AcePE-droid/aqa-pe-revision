## Why Sport and the Law Now Overlap

**Sports law** covers the laws, regulations and judicial decisions governing sport and its athletes. Law and sport were historically separate, but this has changed due to a rise in **deviant acts** in sport, and rising professionalism and commercialisation forcing sport to adhere to legal standards. Four groups are relevant: **performers**, **officials**, **coaches** and **spectators**.

## Legislation and the Performer: Injury and Loss of Earnings

Injuries are usually accepted as an expected side-effect of sport, but a deliberate act to injure another can be illegal.

- A **civil claim** can be brought for injury and/or **loss of earnings**; **damages** require proof of actual injury from an opponent's deliberate, harmful or reckless actions
- The critical test is whether the act fell **outside the playing culture** of the sport – e.g. a foul may be acceptable in football, but a stamp or punch would not be
- Most player-violence issues are handled internally by NGBs, but some lead to prosecution, e.g. Duncan Ferguson (1995, Scotland) and James Cotterill (2006, England) – Matt Holmes is the standard example of a footballer successfully claiming damages for a career-ending tackle

## Legislation and the Performer: Contracts

Performers are **employees** entitled to standard employment rights.

- The **Bosman Ruling (1995)** gave EU footballers the right to move freely to another club at the end of their contract, without a transfer fee
- **Restraint of trade** means action interfering with free competition, e.g. Wayne Rooney's dispute with Proactive Sports Management, where the Court of Appeal held his contract signed aged 17 was unenforceable
- Remember the reasons performers need legal assistance via **SOCCER**: **S**pectators attacking players; **O**pponents being too violent; **C**ontractual issues with sponsors; **C**ontractual issues with employers; **E**quality of opportunity issues; **R**efereeing negligence

## Legislation and Officials: Negligence

**Negligence** is conduct falling below a 'reasonable person standard' that breaches the duty of care and results in foreseeable harm.

- Officials owe participants a **duty of care** to eliminate dangers so the contest takes place in a safe environment
- **Smoldon v Whitworth and Nolan** – claim succeeded, as the referee failed to follow guidelines on collapsed scrums
- **Allport v Wilbraham** – claim failed, as the court preferred the defendant's evidence that the referee had adequately controlled the scrum

## Legislation and Coaches: Duty of Care

**Duty of care** requires a coach to take reasonable measures to keep participants safe – liability arises only where the risk was **foreseeable** but nothing was done about it.

- Coaches are responsible for health and safety (first aid access, emergency contacts), protection from abuse (emotional and physical), and educating athletes about drugs and supplements
- Demonstrating reasonable care with children includes: up-to-date contact/medical details, appropriate supervision ratios, first aid provision, current **DBS clearance**, and risk assessments
- Where children are involved, the coach acts **in loco parentis** – "in the place of a parent"

## Legislation and Spectators: Safety and Hooliganism

Spectators risk prosecution under Public Order Acts for pitch invasion or racist chants. The **Taylor Report** into Hillsborough is the key reference point for tackling hooliganism.

- Measures include all-seater stadia, control of alcohol sales, police-specified kick-off times, increased security and intelligence gathering, and tougher deterrents such as banning orders, fines and imprisonment

## Timeline of Crowd Safety Legislation

1. **Occupiers' Liability Act (1957)** – the occupier of a premises owes a common duty of care to visitors
2. **Safety of Sports Grounds Act (1975)** – protects all spectators, covering all grounds in all sports
3. **Sporting Events (Control of Alcohol etc.) Act (1985)** – banned alcohol possession at or travelling to a football match
4. **Fire Safety and Safety of Place of Sport Act (1987)** – requires a fire safety certificate and sets maximum spectator numbers
5. **Football Spectators Act (1989)** – allows **banning orders** on individuals who have committed offences
6. **Football Offences Act (1991)** – created offences of throwing missiles, racist chanting, and pitch trespass
