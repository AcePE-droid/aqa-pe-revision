## Arousal and Drive Theory

**Arousal** is a state of activation and readiness to perform, ranging on a continuum from very low (sleep-like) to very high (extreme excitement). It can be increased by factors such as the importance of a competition, being watched by an audience (especially experts), and frustration, such as playing badly or losing.

- **Drive theory** proposes that as arousal increases, performance increases in a proportional, linear way:

> P = f(D × H) – Performance is a function of Drive multiplied by Habit

- Drive theory is linked to the **dominant response** – the response a performer believes to be correct. In **experts**, the dominant response is usually correct, so performance remains high even under high arousal. In **beginners**, the dominant response may be underdeveloped, so high arousal increases the risk of poor performance
- Task complexity also matters – **complex tasks** require more information processing and can suffer under high arousal, while **simple tasks** can still be performed well at high arousal levels

## The Inverted-U Theory

The **inverted-U theory** shares drive theory's starting assumption that increasing arousal improves performance, but argues that performance only improves up to an **optimal point**, typically at **moderate arousal** – beyond this point, further arousal causes performance to decline. Both too little and too much arousal are equally damaging to performance.

- The optimal level of arousal is not fixed and varies according to several factors: **experience** – experts can perform well at higher arousal since their dominant response is more likely correct, while novices need lower arousal; **personality** – extroverts tend to perform better at higher arousal while introverts prefer lower arousal, linked to differences in baseline activation levels; and **task type** – gross, simple skills (e.g. rowing) can be performed well at high arousal, while fine, complex skills (e.g. a table tennis return) are better performed at lower arousal

## Catastrophe Theory

**Catastrophe theory** is an adaptation of the inverted-U theory. It agrees that performance peaks at an optimal point of arousal, but argues that beyond this point performance does not gradually decline – instead it drops **suddenly and dramatically**.

- This sudden slump is caused by a combination of high **somatic anxiety** (physiological symptoms, e.g. muscle tension and raised heart rate) and high **cognitive anxiety** (psychological symptoms, e.g. loss of concentration and worry)
- Recovery from the slump is possible if the initial anxiety is mild and the performer has time to regain control; otherwise, panic can cause arousal to rise further, worsening the decline in performance

## The Zone of Optimal Functioning and Peak Flow

The **zone of optimal functioning**, developed by psychologist Hanin, adapts the inverted-U theory further by suggesting that rather than one single optimal point of arousal, each performer has an individual **'zone'** – a bandwidth of arousal within which they perform best.

- This zone can sit at low, moderate or high arousal depending on the individual, and can be found using techniques such as mental practice, relaxation, visualisation and positive self-talk
- **Peak flow** is the ultimate intrinsic experience achievable within the zone, characterised by effortless movement, total focus, supreme confidence and a feeling of complete control – it is promoted by a positive mental attitude, high confidence, relaxation and anxiety control
- Peak flow can be disrupted by factors such as poor mental preparation, environmental pressures (e.g. crowd noise or refereeing decisions), or the effects of injury or fatigue during performance
