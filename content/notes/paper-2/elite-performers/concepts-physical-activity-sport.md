## Physical Recreation

**Physical recreation** sits at the 'foundation' and 'participation' end of the sporting development continuum – it is the active side of leisure, since leisure time is free time that can be spent actively or passively, and when spent actively and voluntarily it becomes physical recreation.

- Its key characteristics are: entirely voluntary and a matter of personal choice; informal and relaxed rather than serious; flexible, since participants can adjust the rules or duration as they wish; self-officiated and self-regulated, with no external officials; and focused on taking part rather than on winning
- For the individual, the benefits can be remembered as five 'S' factors: **self-confidence/self-esteem** from a sense of achievement, **stress reduction**, **skill and fitness development**, improved **social** skills, and a **sense of fun and enjoyment**
- For society, physical recreation reduces strain on the NHS and lowers obesity, increases social integration and community cohesion, creates economic benefits through spending on facilities and equipment, and reduces crime by encouraging positive use of free time – it can also act as a pathway towards performance or elite levels

## Sport: Characteristics and Functions

**Sport** occupies the more serious, competitive end of the continuum – it is highly structured with strict, set rules; requires specialist equipment and kit; is overseen by officials appointed by national governing bodies; involves strategies and tactics to outwit opponents; offers extrinsic (medals, trophies) and intrinsic rewards; demands high skill levels and commitment; and places a strong emphasis on winning.

- For the individual, sport improves health, fitness and skill levels; builds self-confidence through success; provides social opportunities such as teamwork and friendship; and develops positive morals such as fair play and sportsmanship
- For society, the benefits can be remembered as three 'S' and three 'E' factors: reduced **strain** on the NHS, increased **social control** and **social integration**, new **employment** opportunities, **economic** benefits, and greater **equality** of opportunity to take part

## Physical Education: Characteristics and Aims

National Curriculum **Physical Education (PE)** was introduced following the 1988 Education Reform Act, spanning four Key Stages from age 5 to 16 – it is compulsory, formally taught, pre-planned and highly structured, takes place during school time, and is led by teachers.

- PE aims to develop health and fitness and positive attitudes leading to healthy, active lifestyles beyond compulsory schooling; increases participation and physical skills; builds personal and social skills such as teamwork, leadership and co-operation; and instils sporting ethics such as fair play and sportsmanship
- Cognitively, PE develops problem-solving, decision-making and creativity, and encourages pupils to analyse their own performance – the overall intention is to build a sporting 'habit for life'

## Outdoor and Adventurous Activities (OAA)

**OAA** forms part of the National Curriculum PE offer and uses the natural environment as a resource for learning, built around **perceived risk** – a challenge that creates a genuine sense of danger and adventure while actual danger is minimised through strict safety measures, e.g. a harness when climbing.

- The functions of OAA can be remembered using **LEADS**: **L**eadership skills; **E**xcitement/adrenaline rush; **A**ppreciation of the natural environment; **D**ecision-making and cognitive skills; **S**ocial skills and self-esteem
- PE departments can struggle to deliver OAA due to a lack of curriculum time, money for specialist equipment, or qualified staff, a school's location, and health and safety concerns from parents and staff

## School Sport

**School sport** is distinct from PE since it mostly takes place in extra-curricular time and is a matter of choice rather than a compulsory part of the timetable, and is competitive in nature.

- Promoted through government initiatives such as **School Sport Partnerships** (linking secondary schools with primary/junior schools) and the **School Games** (increasing participation from intra-school to national level)
- Participating in school sport brings physical benefits (health, fitness, self-esteem), social benefits (friendships, group membership) and cognitive benefits (improved decision-making, potentially improved academic achievement)

## The Sporting Development Continuum and the Triangular Model of PE

The **sporting development continuum** describes how involvement in physical activity can progress through stages: the **foundation level** (first introduction, typically primary school PE), the **participation level** (emphasis on fun and socialising, where most physical recreation sits), and the **performance level** (genuine commitment to regular participation, where winning becomes important).

- A pupil's overall experience can be represented as a **triangular model** of three linked elements: **Education** (compulsory, formally taught National Curriculum PE), **School Sport** (extra-curricular competitive opportunities, e.g. inter-house fixtures), and **Recreation** (extra-curricular, non-competitive opportunities, e.g. an open aerobics club)

## Comparing the Key Concepts: Similarities and Differences

- **Recreation and sport** are similar in that both improve health and fitness and can produce intrinsic satisfaction – they differ in that recreation is voluntary, participation-focused, flexible and self-officiated with varied skill levels, whereas sport is win-focused, demands high commitment, uses set rules enforced by officials, and typically involves higher skill levels and high-tech equipment
- **Recreation and PE** are similar in that both develop physical skills and are enjoyable – they differ in that recreation is voluntary and informal in a person's own free time, while PE is compulsory, formally taught and teacher-led during school time
- **PE and school sport** contrast clearly: PE is compulsory, applies to all pupils, teacher-led, and emphasises taking part; school sport is voluntary, often more elitist, coach-led, and emphasises winning and competition
