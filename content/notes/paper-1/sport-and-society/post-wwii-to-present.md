## Amateurism and Professionalism Through the Twentieth and Twenty-First Centuries

In the early twentieth century, amateurs remained prominent and typically held higher social status than professionals, often controlling national governing bodies and coming from more privileged, financially secure backgrounds.

As the century progressed, the status gap between amateurs and professionals gradually narrowed, with success increasingly based on ability and achievement rather than social background.

In modern sport the term 'amateur' has become blurred: many top-level amateurs receive significant financial support for training (e.g. National Lottery or Sports Aid funding), raising debate about whether they remain genuinely amateur, while some traditionally amateur sports still have lower-status, unpaid participation at the top level.

Elements of the amateur code have survived into professional sport, e.g. Fair Play awards and pre/post-match handshakes, and the continued use of honorary titles in some sports linked to their amateur heritage.

Factors driving the growth of modern professionalism include all social classes now being able to compete; higher financial rewards and respect through sponsorship and media coverage; more free time for full-time training, raising standards of performance; and the influence of celebrity culture and greater investment in sport, both funding professionals and motivating audiences to follow elite competition.

## Rationalisation and Modern Development of Association Football

Association football developed and modernised due to several combined factors: urbanisation, which created large concentrated audiences and demand for purpose-built stadiums; more free time and disposable income among the working classes (e.g. the Saturday half-day becoming the traditional time for matches); improved transport for travelling to fixtures; and growing professionalism, legalised in England in 1885 once it was clear many players were already being paid unofficially.

Football also became more organised over time, with the Football Association forming in 1863, national rules being codified, and referees taking control of officiating as the game became increasingly commercialised.

Top footballers' wages rose enormously over the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, from an initial maximum wage cap introduced in 1901, through its abolition in 1961, to today's multi-million-pound contracts – partly driven by the 1995 **Bosman ruling**, a European Court of Justice decision giving out-of-contract players freedom of movement between clubs, which significantly increased player bargaining power and wages.

Women's football has grown substantially in professionalism and popularity, supported by legal protection from sex discrimination legislation, growing media coverage (e.g. the Women's Super League and FIFA Women's World Cup), increased FA funding and investment, more grassroots opportunities, and more visible role models in playing, coaching and officiating.

Opportunities for female match officials historically lagged behind the men's game, but structured pathways have since been introduced to progress officials through league levels up to international level.

## Rationalisation and Modern Development of Lawn Tennis

Lawn tennis was invented by the middle classes in the 1870s (patented by Major Walter Clopton Wingfield in 1874) as a more accessible, suburban alternative to real tennis, originally played on courts built in private gardens.

Early lawn tennis reflected its middle-class origins: it required specialist purchased equipment, had standardised written rules from the outset, was played in a socially exclusive and 'polite' manner, and gradually opened up through public provision, e.g. courts built in public parks, widening access beyond the middle classes.

Tennis offered relatively early opportunities for women to take part (from the 1880s), helping challenge some contemporary stereotypes about female participation, although women initially had to play in restrictive clothing.

Tennis remained largely amateur at major tournaments until 1968, when the sport entered the 'open era', allowing professional and amateur players to compete together and earn official prize money for the first time.

The Women's Tennis Association (WTA) has played a major role in developing women's professional tennis, including a high-profile campaign led by Billie Jean King for equal prize money, eventually achieved at Wimbledon and the French Open in 2007.

## Rationalisation and Modern Development of Track and Field Athletics

As with other sports, the rationalisation of athletics was closely tied to urban growth, with events moving from open rural land to purpose-built venues able to host large paying crowds.

The **Amateur Athletic Association (AAA)**, formed in 1880, initially enforced a strict 'exclusion clause' separating wealthier 'amateur' athletes from those who competed for money, excluding professional or manual-working competitors from amateur events.

Women's participation in athletics was historically very restricted: women were not permitted to compete in Olympic athletics until 1928, and were excluded from longer-distance events (e.g. the marathon) at Olympic level until 1984.

Modern athletics is governed internationally by the sport's world governing body, which organises major events and coordinates funding, allowing elite athletes to train and compete full-time as professionals, supported by sponsorship, prize money and media coverage.

## Commercialism, Media and Sponsorship: The Golden Triangle

> The close, mutually dependent relationship between sport, business/sponsorship and the media is often described as the 'golden triangle': each part relies on and benefits the other two. Sport needs media coverage and sponsorship to grow and fund performers; sponsors use sport's audience to advertise; and the media relies on popular sport to attract viewers and advertising revenue.

**Commercialism** describes the process by which sport is increasingly run and promoted with the aim of generating profit, rather than purely for participation or enjoyment.

Modern commercial sport typically shares several features: high standards of skill and performance, close links between sponsorship and business, a strong entertainment focus, valuable contracts and merchandising deals, treatment of top athletes as marketable 'commodities' and role models, and media coverage that is easy for a general audience to follow.

Commercialisation affects professional performers by providing significant income from participation, sponsorship and merchandising; greater financial security; increased pressure to perform and win consistently; the need to specialise fully, often at the cost of privacy; and reduced personal control over their own public image.

Commercialisation has also directly changed the rules and presentation of some sports to make them more attractive to broadcasters and sponsors – for example, shorter formats designed for TV (e.g. Twenty20 cricket), scoring changes to reduce time-wasting (e.g. in badminton), and breaks built into play for advertising.

Women's sport has historically received far less media coverage and sponsorship than men's sport, linked to lower participation, fewer visible role models and less funding reinvested at grassroots and elite level – though coverage and investment have grown considerably in recent years.

The rise of television rights has transformed the finances of major sports since the mid-twentieth century (e.g. English football, broadcast since 1937, has seen the value of live TV rights grow from hundreds of millions to billions of pounds by the 2010s), giving broadcasters increasing influence over how and when events are staged.

More recently, social media has changed the relationship between sport, fans and traditional media: performers and clubs can communicate directly and build large personal followings without relying solely on print or broadcast media, although this also creates new risks around performers' public conduct and reputation online.
